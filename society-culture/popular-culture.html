Popular Culture

Revise popular culture through the teen film focus studied in class.

1. Nature of popular culture

Popular culture refers to cultural products, practices and forms that achieve widespread recognition and consumer access. In the syllabus, popular cultures are distinguished by four characteristics: they are associated with commercial products and paraphernalia, can develop from local to global, achieve widespread consumer access, and are constantly changing and evolving.

Teen Film fits these characteristics because it is tied to commercial film industries, music, fashion and merchandise; has travelled internationally through cinema, television and streaming; reaches broad youth audiences; and continually adapts its representations, technologies and social attitudes.

2. Creation and development

Popular cultures are created within particular social, historical and economic contexts. Teen Film developed strongly in the 1950s United States alongside the emergence of teenagers as a distinct social group, rising disposable income, increased leisure time and expanding youth-focused media.

Development over time involves changes in representation, technology and social attitudes. Rebel Without a Cause can be used for early youth rebellion and identity, while The Breakfast Club demonstrates teen identity groups and the way youth culture becomes a recognisable cultural narrative.

Revision focus: Creation asks where and why the culture began. Development asks how and why it changed over time.
3. Commercialisation and commodification

Commercialisation is the production, marketing and promotion of cultural products for profit. In Teen Film this can include cinema releases, streaming, soundtracks, merchandise, sequels and franchise expansion.

Commodification occurs when cultural ideas, symbols, lifestyles or identities are turned into things that can be bought and sold. High School Musical is useful for commercialisation through Disney, soundtracks, merchandise and franchise expansion, while Clueless demonstrates the commodification of fashion and youth identity.

Exam trap: Do not use commercialisation and commodification as interchangeable terms.
4. From local to global

Popular cultures can spread through globalisation, cultural diffusion, adaptation, Westernisation and technology. Media technologies allow cultural products to cross national borders rapidly, but audiences and industries may adapt them to different cultural expectations.

This means global spread does not necessarily produce identical meanings everywhere. Streaming platforms such as Netflix increase access, while examples such as Squid Game, films altered for Chinese audiences, and the localisation of global brands demonstrate that culture may be changed as it moves between societies.

5. Access, consumption and interpretation

Consumption is shaped by factors such as age, gender, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, location and technology. Cinema, television, streaming and social media have changed when, where and how audiences access film.

Audiences are not simply passive consumers. They interpret popular culture through their own values, experiences and social context. 10 Things I Hate About You (1999) and Do Revenge (2022) are useful comparison points for continuity and change in identity, power, relationships, conflict, representation and gender.

Revision focus: Explain not only who consumes Teen Film, but why patterns of access and interpretation differ.
6. Control, power and consumers

Popular culture is shaped by the interaction between producers, corporations, media organisations, technologies and consumers. Control can occur through ownership, production, distribution, marketing, algorithms and curation.

Disney is useful for examining concentrated ownership and the ability of a corporation to control production and distribution. TikTok's For You Page shows how algorithmic curation influences what users see. At the same time, consumers can actively shape trends, interpretations and cultural production, meaning control is rarely one-directional.

7. Popular culture and social change

Popular culture both reflects and can influence social values. Representations may reinforce existing ideas or challenge norms relating to identity, gender, relationships, diversity and behaviour.

When assessing impact, avoid claiming that a film or platform directly causes social change on its own. Instead, explain how popular culture interacts with audiences, institutions, technologies and wider social trends.

8. Future directions

Future directions should be based on current trends and patterns. Useful trends include streaming, recommendation algorithms, social media, changing values, diversity, gender equality, commercialisation and users becoming both consumers and creators.

Use the sequence trend → impact → future → judgement. Strong responses also identify continuity: popular culture is likely to continue entertaining audiences, reflecting identity, building connection and operating within commercial systems even as technologies and representations change.
